# Business model: one shop's everyday ordering tool

**Next action: choose one reachable neighborhood for interviews — 10 minutes.**

9 September 2026. Everything in this canvas is a proposed business design unless linked to evidence.

## Customer and value proposition

Start with owner-operated cafés, tea shops, snack shops, and small takeaway businesses with one outlet, roughly 20–100 menu items, and staff who already use smartphones. Item counts are a recruitment assumption. Prefer a recurring problem: changing availability, complicated sizes/toppings, missed chat orders, or customers asking for current prices.

The buyer is the owner; the daily user is the cashier or preparer; the customer scans a menu. These people need different things. The owner needs a clear bill and control. Staff need fewer mistakes. Customers need fast browsing without an account.

Avoid early customers who need full inventory costing, fiscal reporting, hardware installation, offline tills, hotel billing, or franchise-system integration. A restaurant already happy with its POS needs a measurable reason to add another screen. Its existing tool may be the better answer.

**Proposed positioning:** “Your own Khmer/English menu and simple order board, set up with you, paid monthly. Your customers pay you.” Use a neutral SaaS name; do not imply franchise authorization to sell Ai-Cha or Zhengda branding to other merchants.

## Compare three offers

| Offer | Benefit | Solo-developer trade-off | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Menu only | Current prices/photos, branding, QR browsing | Few operational failures, but crowded low-price market and weak daily dependence | Test as a $9 self-service fallback; do not build a separate package first |
| Menu + ordering | Structured modifiers and a staff queue; useful daily workflow | Missed orders create real operational harm; requires acceptance, recovery and staff training | **Recommended $19 pilot**, with $29 value test before expanding |
| Menu + ordering + local loyalty | Supports repeat visits to the same business | Identity, refunds, disputes and reward rules add support; repeat-purchase impact unproven | Later optional $5/month price test, merchant pays reward cost |

All proposed prices are assumptions. Ordering is also advertised by cheap competitors, so “we have ordering” is insufficient differentiation. See [competitor evidence](02-competitors.md).

## Why pay instead of the existing alternative?

| Alternative | Where it wins | Our offer has a chance when… |
|---|---|---|
| Paper menu or PDF QR | Familiar, cheap, paper works during outages | Frequent updates and sold-out items cause costly confusion |
| Facebook posts or Telegram chat | Existing audience; familiar conversation | Staff repeatedly retype orders or lose modifier details |
| Existing POS | Already holds sales, kitchen workflow and reporting | This menu complements it without burdensome double entry; otherwise do not sell |
| Free/cheap QR SaaS | Low subscription and easy signup | Owner demonstrably values assisted setup and better daily service enough to pay more |

Do not promise new customers: a QR menu does not supply an audience. Do not claim whole-shop profit from the subset of orders entered into this app.

## Revenue and money ownership

Food money goes from customer to merchant by cash or the merchant's payment arrangement. Software subscription money goes from merchant to your business against a software invoice. The platform records order information but does not receive, pool, or distribute shops' food revenue.

For a later loyalty module, each merchant chooses eligible items and supplies its own reward. Points cannot be purchased, transferred to another merchant, or converted into money. You earn the software fee, not a spread on points. Keep bank charges and reward costs visible as merchant costs, separate from your subscription.

## Reachable market, not an invented national market

Build a local prospect list rather than multiplying national payment QR registrations. **Illustrative funnel:** 60 mapped shops → 20 owner conversations → 8 qualified shops → 3 paid pilots. None are forecast outcomes. At $19, 30 paying locations mean $570 monthly recurring revenue and 100 mean $1,900, before costs. The economics make retention and economical support essential.
